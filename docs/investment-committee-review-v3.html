What the board found

The second review closed with a promise: complete the five-item roadmap and the third review would deliver unconditional sign-off. The board has now re-tested the engine end-to-end against live runs and finds every P0 and P1 item genuinely closed — not claimed closed. The candidate deal ages with the market. BRRR obeys the goal's leverage mandate it once ignored (peak 66% against a 75% ceiling, by construction rather than luck). The min_icr = 0 footgun is dead. Every strategy now renders an IRR with its basis stated (capital-account, on all seven live plans). The single deterministic line has grown a fixed downturn companion and a seeded Monte-Carlo band. Purchases take two months to complete and earn nothing for their first month — the calendar criticism, answered.

The goal engine has moved furthest of all. Where the first review found two duplicate strategies and the second found four with an unbounded risk score reading "0 = riskless", the board now finds seven genuinely distinct plans on a bounded 0–100 risk scale with a per-component breakdown, ranked under a user-owned Fastest/Balanced/Safest policy with the recommendation's provenance stated, a Pareto frontier chart, verified "nearest-feasible" fix hints (every number re-simulated, never extrapolated — the board checked), and two-phase hybrid strategies the second review only sketched. On the 10-property goal, the engine's own hybrid Recycle, Then De-Risk Pareto-dominates plain BRRR — same 75-month goal date, 13 points less risk, £2.0m less ending debt. That is the engine "having the thought" §13 of the second review said it could not have.

What remains is enumerable and honest, and mostly lives at the edges: purchase-tax is charged for the wrong jurisdiction on every simulated purchase (Scottish LBTT/ADS applied uniformly, including to the English seed portfolio — material on 16–24-buy growth plans and, on the report's own £85k deal, an over-charge rather than the omission first suspected), BRRR still lacks the refurbishment-uplift input its economics depend on, per-loan origination LTV is unenforced, and the Goals engine — unlike Scenarios — still presents its pathways as single deterministic lines. None of these changes a headline figure's arithmetic; the jurisdiction mismatch flatters or distorts multi-purchase plans' speed and is priced in §8. (The near-zero-fee finding originally listed here was itself corrected mid-review and fixed at the code level — see §9.)

3Review Methodology

The board reviews the system as configured and as it runs today — prior reviews and their acceptance appendices were read, but every figure in this report was independently regenerated from the live codebase at review time.

What the board ran

5Strategy Analysis

5.1 Maximise Cashflow  Risk 77 · Critical  Engine's recommendation (Fastest mode)

The only plan that reaches £5,000/month with real margin — £9,071 ending post-tax, goal at month 131 — and the engine is now honest about what that costs: £1.78m of interest-only debt across 24 units, the worst equity multiple of the reaching plans (2.67×), and a Critical band driven by a full 15/15 amortisation component. The board accepts the recommendation as a product of the operator's own chosen ranking mode (Fastest); under Safest the same engine recommends Low-Risk Hold, and the toggle plus provenance string make that a user decision rather than a hidden formula. This is the governance the second review demanded. One sensitivity to flag: at £25/£25 configured arrangement/valuation fees (a deliberate, now-defensible operator choice — see §9), 22 purchases cost roughly £28k less than they would at the engine's £999/£300 defaults, which is this plan's cadence advantage most of all — worth restating at defaults if presenting a conservative case.

5.2 Low-Risk Hold  Risk 38 · Medium

The plan most board members would run with their own money, and the numbers have only strengthened: 9 units, £91k residual debt, min ICR 260%, capital efficiency the best of the seven (5.21× on £248k in, 13.6% capital-account IRR, 28.1% ROCE), and goal reached — month 175, inside the horizon, sustained to the end under the corrected goal definition. At the second review this strategy missed by £207/month and the engine had nothing to say about it; the nearest-feasible machinery now closes exactly that class of gap (on the 10-property goal it tells the investor a verified "£15,157 starting cash" flips its near-miss). Slow, boring, and the strongest risk-adjusted return on the table.

5.3 Medium-Risk Hold  Risk 49 · Medium  New since v2

A genuine middle of the frontier rather than a fifth flavour of the same thing: up to 4 concurrent mortgages (operator-configurable; default 3) before de-gearing, landing between Low-Risk Hold and the growth plans on every axis — 11 units, £256k debt, £5,628/month, 4.58×, risk 49. It reaches goal seven months sooner than Low-Risk Hold for 11 points more risk, which is a coherent, explainable trade. The board notes the cap is a Settings value with the 0⇒default guardrail applied — the configuration-surface lesson of §9-v2, learned.

5.4 BRRR  Risk 75 · High

Now mandate-compliant (peak 66% vs the 75% ceiling, capped at source) and feasible — the second review's central BRRR finding is closed. What remains is the economics: 23 purchases funded by 37 refinances produce the worst capital efficiency of the lineup (2.35×, 10.4% IRR, 9.0% ROCE, 25-month payback) because the engine still models no below-market purchase or refurbishment uplift — BRRR without forced appreciation is recycling fees, not value. The board repeats its position: either add a refurb-uplift input (with a matching capex outflow) or let the strategy's own returns block continue to indict it. At least the indictment is now printed on the card.

5.5 The hybrids  Fast Build → Lock In: 62 · High  Recycle → De-Risk: dominates BRRR on goal 2

Fast Build, Then Lock In is the honest cautionary tale on this goal: it converts 13 IO loans to repayment at the goal month, and the conversion's post-tax cost (principal is not deductible) drops income back below target — ending £4,767, correctly marked not reaching goal under the sustained definition, with the £233 shortfall printed. The board specifically verified this case because it is the one a first-crossing definition would have got wrong; the engine got it right. Two residuals: hybrids receive no nearest-feasible hints (the template-probe machinery doesn't cover two-phase plans, so this £233 near-miss shows no fix), and on the 10-property goal Recycle, Then De-Risk demonstrates the concept at full strength — recommended, tied-fastest, 13 points safer than its base.

5.6 Target & Hold  Risk 59 · High

Stops buying at the goal and holds — but on this deal it never quite gets there: £4,564 ending, £436 short, reserve-bound (the cash floor pinches at £16.6k vs a £15.9k requirement), with a verified hint that £40,000 starting cash (vs £15,000) flips it. Sixteen repayment mortgages held simultaneously with no de-gearing gives it more mid-life leverage than its "steady" branding suggests — the 59 High score is deserved, and the breakdown shows why (leverage 12.2, affordability 18.6, scale 10). A professional would either fund the hint or pick Medium-Risk Hold.

6Financial Logic & Internal Consistency

The board re-traced the cash engine and found it internally consistent for the third consecutive review — and, for the first time, found the strategy layer's arithmetic equally clean. Findings, in descending severity:

6.1 Purchase tax is charged for the wrong jurisdiction on every simulated purchase Medium

Deposits, legal, arrangement and valuation fees, ERC, capex and arrears all flow through the cash account — and so, in fact, does purchase tax: calculations.ts implements Scotland's Land and Buildings Transaction Tax (calcLBTT(), banded 0/2/5/10/12%) and its 8% Additional Dwelling Supplement (calcADS()), and completePurchase() deducts their combined total on every purchase, tested and regression-protected. This report's first draft stated the opposite — that stamp duty was entirely absent — which was wrong; the correction is recorded in §1.

The real defect: there is no jurisdiction switch anywhere in the codebase. Scottish LBTT/ADS is charged unconditionally, including on the seed portfolio's own stock (Stockport, Leeds, Sheffield, Manchester — all England), which should attract English SDLT with the 5% additional-property surcharge introduced in the October 2024 Budget, not Scottish rates. On the report's own £85,000 candidate deal: currently charged £6,800 (LBTT £0, below the £145k nil-band, + ADS 8% = £6,800) versus £4,250 if correctly charged as English SDLT (the whole price sits inside SDLT's 0% band; only the 5% surcharge applies) — an over-charge of ~£2,550 per purchase on this specific deal, the opposite direction from the omission first suspected. The sign is price-band-dependent (LBTT's nil-band sits higher than SDLT's, but ADS's 8% exceeds SDLT's 5% surcharge), so it should not be assumed to run one way across all price points — which is exactly why a proper jurisdiction switch, not a guess, is the fix. It does not corrupt the ledger (nothing double-counts; the wrong figure is still applied consistently within a run), but it does misstate acquisition costs on English/Welsh/NI stock in a direction the board cannot sign off as immaterial on a 16–24-purchase plan. Fix: a jurisdiction field (England/Wales/NI vs Scotland) selecting between the existing calcLBTT/calcADS and a new SDLT/LTT schedule, applied in completePurchase() alongside the existing fee hooks — closing the "SDLT/LTT jurisdiction switch" half of the second review's own deferred item 7, not a new defect.

6.2 Payback period can render as 0 months Low

On several 10-property-goal plans months_to_payback reports 0: the cash-position trough sits at month 0, so "recovery to starting position" is satisfied immediately even though £250k+ of capital is deployed later. The trough-to-recovery definition is defensible, but a 0-month payback on a quarter-million-pound plan reads as an error to a professional. Cosmetic; worth a definitional guard (e.g. measure from the last capital call, or suppress when the trough precedes first purchase).

Correction to this finding — the £25 was a workaround for a real bug, not an operator choice to game a warning threshold

This report originally framed the configured £25/£25 fees as an operator exploiting the fact that the P0-3 prudence warning only fires at exactly zero — and recommended widening the warning band (§14 item 2, as first drafted) as the fix. The operator corrected the board on this point: 0 was the value they actually wanted (several BTL fee structures are genuinely fee-free), but entering it triggered a defect — positiveOr(), the same guardrail correctly built to stop min_icr: 0 silently disabling a lender-affordability gate, was also applied to the three transaction-cost fields, where a stored zero is a legitimate value, not a blank field or a mistake. Every layer, including the global Settings default, silently rewrote a configured 0 to the engine literal (£999/£300/£2,000) with no way to express a genuine zero fee at all — £25 was the only workaround available. Widening the warning band, as first recommended, would have made this worse: it would flag the honest workaround while the real zero remained unreachable. The actual fix, now shipped: the three fee fields (arrangement_fee, valuation_fee, legal_fees) now use a separate numberOr() helper that only falls through on null/unset, not on 0 — leaving positiveOr()'s 0⇒default behaviour untouched for the genuine safety gates (min_icr, capex/cash reserves) where a real lender or reserve requirement is never zero. §14 item 2 is retitled accordingly and closed.

Would a lender accept these assumptions? The stress methodology, yes — unchanged and PRA-realistic. The configured arrears value, no — an underwriter would restate it to the default; the configured fees are now a defensible operator choice rather than a modelling gap, given a genuine £0/£25 is achievable without a workaround. Would an experienced investor rely on the engine? With arrears restored and the jurisdiction switch corrected: yes, as a central case with a stated range — and for the first time the range genuinely exists (downturn + Monte-Carlo, in Scenarios).

Is the What-If engine now suitable for supporting investment decisions?
