1Executive Summary

The platform has built a genuinely excellent financial modelling engine on top of almost no data. That is the whole product review in one sentence, and the £500,000 should be spent correcting the imbalance rather than deepening it.

Three consecutive investment-committee reviews have hardened the scenario and goal engines to a standard I have rarely seen in this category: a pure, tested projection function; seven distinct generated strategies including two-phase hybrids; a bounded 0–100 risk score whose ordering matches an expert panel's independent assessment; seeded Monte-Carlo bands; a standing downturn case; verified nearest-feasible fix hints. Measured as modelling software, this is ahead of every UK competitor I have benchmarked, including PropertyData.

Measured as an intelligence product, it barely exists. The audit finding that should reframe the next twelve months is this: the market_data table is declared in the schema and is read by nothing. Not one route, not one service, not one component. Every sophisticated projection the engine produces is driven by a single candidate deal that the investor typed in by hand — one price, one rent, one yield, cloned sixteen to twenty-four times and indexed forward at an assumed growth rate. The engine can tell you, to the month, when a hand-typed assumption reaches £5,000/month. It cannot tell you whether that assumption is available anywhere in Britain.

The central strategic finding

The product's modelling sophistication has outrun its inputs by roughly two years of development. Adding an eighth strategy, a finer risk decomposition or a better IRR fallback compounds a diminishing return. The binding constraint on investor value is no longer how well we model a deal — it is that we have no idea what deals exist. Until a market data layer lands, every improvement to the engine is leverage applied to a guess.

The second finding is commercial rather than technical, and it is a hard gate. The platform is a single-user, locally-hosted application: SQLite on the filesystem, no users table, no authentication, no tenancy boundary, no billing. There is no sense in which it can currently take money from anyone. "Commercial viability" is not a feature on this roadmap — it is a prerequisite that must be built before any feature can be sold, and it is unglamorous enough that it will be deferred forever unless it is explicitly funded. I have funded it.

The third finding is the opportunity. The competitive map (§3) has a hole in exactly the shape of this product. PropertyData knows the market but not your portfolio. Landlord Vision, Hammock and Portfolio Planner know your portfolio but not the market. Rightmove and Zoopla own the audience but serve buyers, not investors. Nobody credibly joins market intelligence to portfolio-level financial modelling — and the join is worth more than either half, because it is the only way to answer the question every investor actually asks, which is not "what is Sunderland's yield" but "given what I already own, what should I buy next, and what does it do to my plan?" The engine to answer the second half is already built and paid for. The first half is what £500,000 buys.

2.3 Reading the pattern

Lay the two tables side by side and the shape is unmistakable. Everything the product does well operates on numbers the investor supplied. Everything it does badly or not at all requires knowing something about the world outside the investor's own records. The team has repeatedly chosen depth over breadth at exactly the point where breadth was the constraint — and the review process reinforced it, because three consecutive due-diligence exercises graded the engine's internal consistency without ever asking whether its inputs were real.

The uncomfortable question the roadmap must answer

A goal plan currently states that buying an £85,000 property yielding 9.9% gross, twenty-two times over, reaches £5,000/month in month 131. Every step of that arithmetic has been verified to a professional standard. But the plan never asks whether twenty-two such properties exist, whether they exist in one town, whether that town's rents are rising or falling, or whether the yield survives the next tenant. An investor acting on this plan is taking a market view the software has no opinion on — while the software's polish implies that it does. That gap between apparent and actual authority is a product risk as much as a feature gap.

2.4 The user we are actually building for

The primary user — UK, limited company, three properties, growing to ten, long-term, data-driven — is well chosen, and worth stating plainly because it disqualifies several tempting directions. This investor is not a developer (so land, planning and GDV tooling is out of scope), not a flipper (so refurb-and-sell velocity tooling is secondary), and not a letting agent (so tenancy operations at scale are not the wedge). They are a portfolio builder making perhaps two to four acquisitions a year, each worth £60k–£250k, each irreversible for a decade.

That cadence has a sharp product implication. The investor uses the modelling engine a handful of times a year, at the moment of a decision. That is not enough to build a habit, and habit is what a subscription is sold on. Market intelligence, watchlists and alerts are used weekly — they are the retention layer that makes the annual decision moment monetisable. This is the same structural reason Bloomberg sells a terminal rather than a valuation model: the analytics justify the price, but the live data justifies the login.

3.3 Where we win, and where we are exposed

Defensible advantages

  • The projection engine is two years ahead of the nearest portfolio competitor and is architecturally clean enough to extend cheaply. Rebuilding it is a serious undertaking, which is the definition of a moat.
  • Institutional-grade credibility. Lender ICR gating, stress testing, tax-structure awareness and honest uncertainty labelling are rare in this market, where most tools quote gross yield and stop.
  • The join is unclaimed. No competitor connects market signals to a personalised portfolio plan. Occupying it first, with the engine already built, is a genuine first-mover position.

Exposures

  • PropertyData could add portfolio modelling faster than we can add data. They already own the harder half. This is the single largest competitive risk in the plan and it argues for speed over polish on the data layer.
  • No distribution. Rightmove and Zoopla own the investor's first search. We have no top-of-funnel and no acquisition channel budgeted beyond the product itself.
  • Rental data has no free authoritative source at the granularity investors expect. This is a licensing cost and a commercial dependency, not an engineering problem (§11).
  • Single-user architecture means every month before the commercial foundation lands is a month of zero revenue and zero market feedback.

Positioning statement

For UK portfolio investors building toward a defined income or property-count goal, who must choose where to deploy each next £50k of capital, Portfolio Intelligence is the only platform that scores the market through the lens of your own portfolio, leverage and tax position — telling you not merely which areas are performing, but which purchase, in which postcode, gets your plan to your goal fastest, and what it does to your risk. Unlike PropertyData, it knows what you own; unlike Landlord Vision and Portfolio Planner, it knows what the market is doing.

4The Strategic Thesis

What "Bloomberg Terminal for UK property investors" actually commits us to — and what it rules out.

4.1 What Bloomberg actually is

The analogy is invoked loosely in this market, usually to mean "a lot of charts on a dark background". It is worth being precise, because the real structure of the Bloomberg product is a very specific and instructive three-layer stack:

The instructive part is that Bloomberg's analytics are, in isolation, unremarkable; a competent quant can reproduce most of them. What cannot be reproduced is the data estate underneath and the workflow habit on top. We have inverted this: our analytics are the hardest part of our product to copy, and we have neither of the two layers that actually retain users. The twelve-month plan is, in essence, building layers 1 and 3 around an already-excellent layer 2.

4.2 The thesis in one diagram

Market signal → personalised interpretation → plan impact → action.

Every competitor stops after the first arrow. PropertyData tells you Sunderland yields 9.1%. It cannot tell you that, given your £47k available cash, your 145% company ICR floor, your existing £312k of debt and your £5,000/month goal, a Sunderland purchase brings your goal fourteen months closer while adding six points of risk — and that a Newcastle purchase at 6.2% does the opposite. 5.2 Missing functionality — the deal supply problem

The single most valuable change to the What-If engine is not a modelling change

Every strategy buys clones of one hand-typed candidate deal. A 24-purchase pathway assumes twenty-four identical £85,000 properties yielding 9.9%, all available on demand, all in an unnamed location, differing only by an indexed price. No real portfolio was ever built this way.

Recommendation — Deal Pipeline Abstraction (P0). Replace the single candidate deal with a deal set: several archetypes, each with price, rent, expense profile, area, availability rate and yield distribution — sourced from the market data layer (§6) or the user's own pipeline. The generator then chooses which deal to buy at each decision point, not merely whether. This single change converts the engine from a calculator into an allocator, and it is the prerequisite for opportunity discovery (§7) to feed back into goal planning.

5.5 Missing risk analysis

  • Downturn is not applied to goal pathways. The Safest ranking mode ranks on central-case numbers only — a "safest" plan that has never been stress-tested is safest by construction, not by evidence. The transform exists and is pure; this is a caller-side change and it is the highest-leverage risk item on the list.
  • No correlation modelling. Voids, arrears, price falls and rate rises are simulated independently. In a real downturn they arrive together. Even a crude correlation matrix would materially improve the downturn case's honesty.
  • No concentration risk on planned purchases. Concentration warnings cover the held portfolio only; a plan that buys 24 clones in one town raises no flag — the single largest unpriced risk in any generated pathway.
  • No lender-appetite or refinance-availability risk. BRRR assumes 37 refinances all complete at the assumed valuation. There is no probability that a lender declines, or that a valuation comes in short — the most common real-world failure of that strategy.
  • No personal/covenant risk. Income loss, s455 exposure on director loans, personal guarantee triggers. The plans assume the investor's own circumstances never change.

5.6 Missing comparisons

Comparison is where an intelligence product proves its worth, and it is currently the thinnest surface in an otherwise deep engine. Five comparisons are missing, in value order:

  1. Versus the market — "your Stockport house yields 6.2%; the SK postcode median is 7.1%." Requires §6, delivers the single most-requested feature in this category.
  2. Versus doing nothing — every plan should be benchmarked against holding the current portfolio untouched. Some plans will lose to it; the investor deserves to know which.
  3. Versus other asset classes — an honest comparison against a passive index or gilt yield over the same horizon, risk-adjusted. Bloomberg-grade credibility; nobody in UK proptech does it.
  4. Versus your own past plan — plan vs actual drift. The investor set a goal a year ago; are they on track? This is the retention hook hiding inside the goal engine.
  5. Versus peer portfolios — anonymised cohort benchmarking. Only viable at scale, but it becomes a compounding data moat once user count is meaningful.

Net recommendation for the What-If engine

£40,000 — the smallest allocation of the five workstreams, deliberately. Spend it on the deal pipeline abstraction, sell-and-replace with CGT, the refurb model and the jurisdiction fix — then stop. The engine's marginal return per pound is now lower than the data layer's, and will stay lower until the data layer exists. The right posture for the next twelve months is to feed this engine, not extend it.

6Market Intelligence Module — Complete Design

The largest single investment in the plan: £190,000, roughly 38% of budget. This is the layer the product does not have and cannot buy its way around.

6.1 Design principles

  1. Postcode district is the unit of analysis. Outward-code granularity (SR1, NE6, DH4) is how investors actually think, is small enough to be actionable and large enough to have statistically meaningful sample sizes. Full postcodes are too sparse for rents; local authorities are too coarse to be useful.
  2. Every metric is a time series, never a snapshot. The existing market_data table's fatal design flaw is one row per area with a single price_growth_1yr column. Trend is the product — "which areas are improving" is unanswerable without history. Rebuild as an append-only monthly observation table.
  3. Provenance and freshness are first-class. Every figure carries its source, observation date and sample size, and the UI shows them. An intelligence product that cannot say where a number came from is a rumour engine.
  4. Confidence is displayed, not hidden. A yield computed from 4 rental comps is not the same as one from 400. Show the sample count; suppress or grey metrics below a threshold rather than printing a confident-looking number.
  5. Derived signals are versioned. Scores and forecasts change methodology over time; stamp them (AREA_SCORE_V1) exactly as the downturn transform already is, so historical outputs stay reproducible.

6.3 Data model

Four new tables, replacing the dead market_data stub. The append-only observation design is the critical choice — it makes every trend, forecast and alert computable from one table without destructive updates.

areas — the canonical registry. Postcode district, region, local authority, centroid lat/long, population, dwelling count, active flag. One row per district (~2,900 in the UK), effectively static.

area_observations — the heart of the module. Append-only: (area_id, metric_key, period, value, sample_size, source_id, confidence, ingested_at). Every metric in §6.2 for every area for every period lands here. Unique on (area_id, metric_key, period, source_id) so re-ingestion is idempotent. Indexed on (area_id, metric_key, period) for trend queries.

7.2 The pipeline

Stage 1 — Universe. All ~2,900 postcode districts with sufficient data confidence.

Stage 2 — Hard filters. The investor's non-negotiables: maximum purchase price (derived from available cash and deposit percentage), minimum yield, regions in or out, property types, maximum distance from home or an existing holding, minimum data confidence.

Stage 3 — Strategy scoring. Re-weight the Area Score (§6.4) to the investor's actual strategy. An income investor targeting £5,000/month weights yield and yield-stability; a capital-growth investor weights growth, momentum and fundamentals; a BRRR investor weights the price-to-refurbished-value spread and stock age.

Stage 4 — Portfolio-aware adjustment. Where it becomes ours alone. Penalise districts that worsen geographic concentration; penalise those correlated with existing holdings (same employer base, same tenant demographic); flag Article 4 and licensing regimes that affect the investor's specific property type; adjust for the tax structure they actually use.

Stage 5 — Goal simulation. The differentiator. For each of the top ~20 candidate areas, construct a representative deal from that area's real medians (price, rent, expected void, expense ratio), inject it into the goal engine as a candidate, re-run pathway generation, and record months-to-goal, resulting risk score and end equity. Rank by goal impact, not by yield.

Stage 5 is computationally the expensive part and it is already solved: buildProjection() is pure and fast, and the nearest-feasible-fix machinery already re-simulates candidate variations rather than extrapolating them. This is the same pattern applied to areas instead of parameters — genuinely a caller-side extension of code that exists and is tested.

8Area Tracking, Watchlists & Alerts

The cheapest module in the plan and the most important for retention. Analytics justify the subscription price; this is what makes the investor log in on a Tuesday.

8.1 Why this matters disproportionately

Our user makes two to four purchase decisions a year. A product used four times a year does not sustain a monthly subscription — the investor cancels in month three and returns when they next need it, if they remember it exists. Watchlists and alerts convert an episodic decision tool into a weekly habit, and they do so at a fraction of the cost of any other module here. Every subscription analytics business — Bloomberg, Morningstar, PropertyData alike — is built on this asymmetry.

Alert fatigue is the failure mode

A product that emails an investor daily about statistical noise gets filtered within a fortnight and never recovers. Three design rules, from the outset: (1) default to weekly batching, not real-time; (2) suppress triggers that fall inside the metric's own confidence interval — if the yield moved 0.3% and the standard error is 0.5%, that is not an event; (3) cap volume per user per week and rank by materiality, sending the top N rather than all. Retention is measured on the digest's open rate, and open rate dies of over-sending long before it dies of under-sending.

9.2 Portfolio Health & Valuation

Market-linked revaluation — the highest value-per-pound item in the entire plan

Portfolio equity is the platform's headline number, and it is currently driven by manually-entered valuations that decay from the day they are typed. Once area price indices exist, every property can be indexed monthly from its last known valuation or purchase price, giving a continuously current, defensible equity figure with a stated confidence band and provenance. It is roughly three engineer-weeks on top of data the module already ingests, it improves the accuracy of every downstream metric at once — LTV, ROCE, equity multiple, risk scoring, refinance detection — and it visibly changes the dashboard on day one for a user who has bought nothing. If only one thing from §9 ships, it is this.

  • Benchmarked scorecard — every health metric expressed against its market, not in isolation. "Your 6.2% yield ranks in the 38th percentile for SK1" is a fundamentally different statement from "your yield is 6.2%", and it is the difference between a dashboard and an intelligence product.
  • Portfolio-level market exposure — which area factors the portfolio is actually levered to (one employer, one tenant demographic, one transport corridor). The concentration service exists but reasons about towns and types, not economic drivers.
  • Equity release capacity — continuously computed headroom to the investor's LTV mandate across the whole portfolio, using live indexed valuations. Answers "what could I raise if I wanted to?" without running a scenario.

Assessed sceptically, because this category attracts more budget than it returns. My position: AI is a presentation and pattern layer over a rigorous engine — never a replacement for it, and never the thing we sell.

The governing principle

This product's competitive advantage is that its numbers are defensible — every figure traceable to a tested calculation with stated assumptions. Every figure in AI-generated text is interpolated from an engine output, and the underlying calculation stays one click away.

11.2 Pipeline architecture

Six stages, each independently monitored: ingest (per-source adapters on schedule) → normalise (to the common observation shape) → geocode & join (everything onto the postcode spine) → aggregate (to district, with sample counts) → derive (yields, trends, scores, forecasts) → publish (versioned, timestamped, exposed to the app).

Non-negotiables, learned from the schema audit that found a dead table: idempotent re-ingestion (same input, same output, safe to replay); immutable observations (corrections are new rows with later ingested_at, never overwrites); a source health dashboard visible to the team from week one; and automated freshness alerts — a silently stale feed is worse than a missing one, because the UI keeps presenting it with confidence.

11.3 Coverage & quality

Coverage will be uneven and the product must say so rather than paper over it. Rural districts will have thin rental samples; some metrics will lag by a quarter. Three rules: publish sample size alongside every metric; suppress rather than estimate below a confidence floor (an honest "insufficient data" outperforms a confident wrong number, and is far cheaper to recover from); and reconcile derived figures against official indices quarterly, publishing the variance internally as a data-quality KPI.

11.4 Legal & licensing

Do not build on scraped portal data

Scraping Rightmove or Zoopla listings breaches their terms of use and creates an existential single point of failure: the product dies the day a block or a letter arrives, and it is unsellable and un-fundable in due diligence with that dependency in it. Every rental and listing input must be either open-licensed or contractually licensed. This is why £25k–£60k/year of rental-comp licensing appears in the budget rather than a fortnight of scraper engineering — it is the difference between an asset and a liability, and the cheaper path is the one that ends the company.

Also required: attribution compliance for Open Government Licence sources; UK GDPR review before any personal or address-level data is retained (EPC and Price Paid both warrant a documented lawful-basis assessment); and a standing policy that redistribution terms are checked before any licensed figure is exposed through an export or API.

11.5 The compounding moat

Every source in Tier 1 is available to any competitor for free, and Tier 2 to anyone with a chequebook. Neither is a moat — they are the entry fee. Three things compound and cannot be bought:

  1. Time series depth. Ingestion started today produces a five-year history in five years. A competitor starting in year three is permanently three years behind on every back-test and every model. This is the single strongest argument for beginning ingestion in month one even before the UI exists.
  2. Methodology, validated in public. A published, back-tested Area Score and forecast accuracy record becomes a reference point competitors are measured against — the Morningstar-rating position.
  3. Outcome data. Uniquely, we observe what users modelled, what they bought, and how it actually performed. That closed loop — prediction against realised outcome — is the foundation of a genuinely superior recommendation model and is structurally unavailable to any pure data vendor or any pure portfolio tracker. It is the reason to be both.

  • Broker referral — the refinance radar (§9.4) identifies a qualified, timed, high-intent lead as a by-product of doing its job. Properly disclosed and never allowed to influence ranking, this is a clean and material revenue line.
  • Data / API licensing — once the Area Score has a published track record, accountants, brokers and smaller proptech tools become buyers of the derived layer.
  • White-labelled reports — per-report or bundled, sold through the Adviser tier.
  • Explicitly declined: deal-sourcing commissions and lead-sale to agents. Both compromise the independence that makes the recommendations trustworthy, and trustworthiness is the product.

12.4 Unit economics sketch

At £39/month, the £190,000 data layer needs roughly 410 paying Investor-tier subscribers to cover its annual cost — before engineering. That is the number the whole plan turns on, and it has two implications worth stating plainly. First, data cost is largely fixed while revenue is linear: the same ingestion serves 400 users or 40,000, so the model is unattractive below roughly 500 subscribers and strongly attractive above 2,000. Second, it argues for launching the paid tier early and narrow rather than late and complete — real subscribers in month six, even at low volume, tell us whether the £39 price holds while there is still budget left to respond.

16If I Could Only Build Five Things

The question that forces the strategy to declare itself. Ranked 1–5, with what each costs, what it unlocks, and what I would sacrifice to protect it.

🎯

Three of the five are data or distribution. One is commercial plumbing. Only one touches the modelling engine.

That distribution is the entire recommendation of this review. The engine is the best thing about this product and it is not what should be built next.

1  The Market Data Layer

£190,000 · months 1–6 · §6, §11

Postcode-district prices, rents, yields, growth, demographics, employment, crime, schools and infrastructure — ingested monthly, versioned, with provenance and confidence on every figure. The market_data table that has been sitting in the schema unread since the product was built, made real.

Why first: it is the binding constraint on everything else. Opportunity discovery, area tracking, benchmarking, revaluation, forecasting and the year-two ML model are all strictly downstream of it — none can start without it and all become straightforward once it exists. It is also the only item whose value compounds with time: every month of delay permanently costs a month of history that cannot be purchased retroactively (§11.5).

What I would sacrifice for it: every remaining item on the engine backlog, without hesitation.

2  Opportunity Discovery, Tied To The Goal Engine

£95,000 · months 4–9 · §7

Ranked areas matched to the investor's strategy and constraints, each candidate simulated through their actual portfolio and goal: "SR1 brings your £5,000/month goal fourteen months closer, adds six points of risk, and improves your geographic concentration."

Why second: this is the product's reason to exist. It is the only feature in this document that no competitor can build without first building both halves of our product — PropertyData would need our engine, Portfolio Planner would need our data. It converts the existing modelling investment from an impressive calculator into a decision system, and it is the sentence the company should be described by.

Dependency: requires #1 and the deal pipeline abstraction (§5.2) — which is the one piece of engine work in the top five, and only because it feeds this.

3  The Commercial Foundation

£85,000 · months 2–6 · §12

Multi-tenancy, authentication, hosted deployment, billing, onboarding. A second person can log in; a first person can pay.

Why third: it scores 1–3 out of 10 on investor value and 10 out of 10 on revenue potential, and that asymmetry is precisely why it needs protecting. Every pound spent on features 1, 2, 4 and 5 returns exactly zero revenue until this exists, and it is the kind of work that gets deferred quarter after quarter because no user ever asks for it. Naming it in the top five is the only reliable way to ensure it ships. Sequencing it with the PostgreSQL migration that #1 requires saves roughly £15,000 of duplicated database work.

Secondary benefit: real subscribers by month six means real pricing feedback while there is still budget left to act on it.

4  Area Watchlists & Alerts

~£28,000 · months 3–6 · §8

Saved areas, threshold and trend alerts, portfolio drift warnings, and a weekly email digest.

Why fourth: the cheapest item in the top five by an order of magnitude, and the one that determines whether subscriptions survive month three. Our investor makes two to four decisions a year — a product used four times a year cannot sustain a monthly subscription. Watchlists and the weekly digest convert an episodic tool into a weekly habit, which is the mechanism every subscription analytics business in this category actually runs on. The digest alone is roughly two engineer-weeks and scores 10/10 on revenue potential.

Watch for: alert fatigue is the failure mode. Batch weekly, suppress noise inside confidence intervals, cap volume (§8.3).

5  Market-Linked Portfolio Valuation & Benchmarking

~£30,000 · months 2–4 · §9.2

Every property indexed monthly against its area's price index; every portfolio metric expressed as a percentile against its market rather than in isolation.

Why fifth — and why it is not lower: it is the best value-per-pound item in the entire plan (9/10 investor value against 2/10 effort). It makes the market data earn its keep on day one for an investor who buys nothing, replacing a headline equity figure that decays from the moment it is typed with a continuously current, defensible one — and simultaneously improving the accuracy of LTV, ROCE, equity multiple, risk scoring and refinance detection, all of which read from valuations. It is also the fastest demonstration that the market↔portfolio join is real, which is what the 90-day proof point (§14) turns on.

Ranked fifth only because it is strictly downstream of #1 and cheap enough to fit alongside the others rather than compete with them.

Closing position

This product has spent its development so far building the half of Bloomberg that Bloomberg finds easiest — the analytics — and none of the half that Bloomberg actually sells, which is the data and the daily habit around it. That inversion is why the platform can model a twenty-four property acquisition programme to the month, and cannot tell its owner whether a single one of those properties exists.

The £500,000 should be spent correcting that inversion. Build the data layer, join it to the engine that already works, make the result sellable, and give the investor a reason to log in on a Tuesday. Do those five things and the vision stops being an analogy: an investor opens the platform, sees what the market did this week, sees what it did to their portfolio, and sees which postcode gets them to £5,000 a month fastest — with the arithmetic behind every figure one click away. That product does not exist in the UK today, and every component required to build it is either already written or costed in this document.
